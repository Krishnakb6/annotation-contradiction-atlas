## Abstract

Biomedical knowledge discovery relies heavily on curated sequence databases such as UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot and the Gene Ontology (GO). However, the exponential growth of biological sequence data has outpaced manual experimental characterization, causing databases to depend increasingly on computational inference and automated propagation pipelines. When automated transfers propagate without rigorous verification, annotation inconsistencies and misannotations accumulate across public repositories. In this work, we present the **Annotation Contradiction Atlas**, an open-source database and evidence-aware decision-support framework designed to prioritize potentially contradictory functional annotations in human protein kinases. Rather than functioning as an automated oracular classifier, the Atlas operates as a transparent triage assistant for expert biocurators. 

The framework integrates four distinct computational evidence layers: (1) global sequence similarity via Needleman-Wunsch pairwise alignment and $k$-mer Jaccard overlap, (2) dense protein language model representations generated by the 320-dimensional `esm2_t6_8M_UR50D` transformer coupled with sliding-window pooling and class-conditioned centroid/$k$-nearest-neighbor anomaly scoring, (3) structural kinase-specific catalytic motif verification targeting canonical subdomains (VAIK, HRD, DFG) with strict spatial order constraints, and (4) annotation provenance reliability scoring parameterized by UniProt evidence ontology codes and literature citations. 

We rigorously evaluated the system on an audited 44-case benchmark (Benchmark v2), strictly partitioned into 21 calibration cases, 21 independent test cases (spanning synthetic label swaps and catalytic motif knockouts), and two natural biological exceptions (the human pseudokinases VRK3 and ILK). On the independent test partition ($N=21$), the global sequence similarity baseline achieved the highest overall performance ($F_1 = 0.6000$, Precision $= 0.7500$, Recall $= 0.5000$, Specificity $= 0.7778$), outperforming both the multi-evidence ensemble ($F_1 = 0.2667$) and ESM-2 anomaly scoring alone ($F_1 = 0.2667$, with 1 false positive and 10 false negatives at the calibrated threshold $\tau = 0.1814$). Diagnostic sensitivity sweeps revealed that global mean-pooled ESM-2 embeddings dilute localized single-motif perturbations across long sequence lengths, yielding zero positive predictions at thresholds $\tau \ge 0.40$. Furthermore, we demonstrate that natural pseudokinases (VRK3 diagnostic score $0.4537$; ILK diagnostic score $0.7167$) must be segregated into an independent diagnostic review queue to prevent legitimate evolutionary adaptations from polluting suspected misannotation triage queues. 

Our findings underscore that high computational discordance does not equate to database error, highlight the nuanced limitations of generalized language model representations for localized enzymatic active-site verification, and establish the necessity of provenance-aware, human-in-the-loop triage systems in modern biocuration workflows.

**Index Terms**—Annotation contradiction, biocuration triage, protein kinases, pseudokinases, protein language models, ESM-2, catalytic motifs, evidence provenance, biological databases.

## I. Introduction

Biological sequence repositories are the foundational pillars of modern bioinformatics, molecular biology, and computational pharmacology. Curated knowledge bases such as the Universal Protein Resource (UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot) \cite{uniprot2023, uniprot2025} and the Gene Ontology (GO) \cite{go2023} synthesize decades of empirical biochemical research into structured, machine-actionable annotations spanning molecular functions, catalytic activities, subcellular localizations, and phenotypic associations. However, the relentless acceleration of high-throughput genomic and transcriptomic sequencing has produced an unprecedented data imbalance: while UniProtKB/TrEMBL contains hundreds of millions of unreviewed computational sequence translations, Swiss-Prot contains fewer than 600,000 manually reviewed entries. To bridge this vast gulf, automated annotation transfer systems (such as UniRule, ARBA, and homology-based inheritance) are routinely deployed to populate functional descriptions across uncharacterized proteins.

While computational transfer is indispensable, it introduces severe risks of systematic error propagation \cite{brenner1999, devos2001, schnoes2009}. When a protein sequence is annotated based on transitive homology or partial sequence alignment, errors in initial database entries or imprecise boundary definitions can propagate unchecked through secondary and tertiary repositories \cite{jones2007}. In particular, enzymes sharing moderate sequence similarity may diverge substantially in substrate specificity, metal-binding requirements, or catalytic competence. A pervasive failure mode occurs when a sequence retains the overall structural scaffold of an enzyme family but has lost essential catalytic machinery through evolutionary subfunctionalization or pseudogenization \cite{eyers2013, murphy2014}. 

Protein kinases represent one of the most prominent and clinically consequential enzyme superfamilies in the human genome, comprising over 500 members that regulate virtually every eukaryotic signaling pathway through reversible phosphorylation \cite{manning2002}. Due to their central role in oncology, immunology, and metabolic disease, kinase functional annotations directly guide experimental target validation, chemical screening, and clinical trials. Yet kinases also present formidable annotation challenges. The human kinome contains an estimated 50 to 60 non-catalytic pseudokinases \cite{eyers2016, jacobsen2019}—proteins that maintain a canonical eukaryotic protein kinase (ePK) fold but lack one or more invariant catalytic residues required for phosphotransfer (e.g., the catalytic lysine in subdomain II, the catalytic aspartate in subdomain VIB, or the metal-chelating aspartate in subdomain VII). Automated pipelines frequently misclassify pseudokinases as active kinases, or conversely, computational consistency checkers flag legitimate pseudokinases as corrupted records because their sequences diverge from canonical active-site expectations.

To address these challenges, existing computational quality-control pipelines typically deploy rule-based filters, sequence-homology thresholds, or machine learning anomaly detectors. However, most existing tools treat annotation error detection as an autonomous, binary classification task: an entry is either flagged as "correct" or "erroneous." In real-world biocuration, this framing is fundamentally flawed for three major reasons:

1. **Computational discordance is not equivalent to database error:** A protein sequence may exhibit atypical sequence or structural features due to genuine evolutionary specialization, lineage-specific divergence, or alternate isoform splicing, rather than a curation mistake.
2. **Global representations mask localized enzymatic functional loss:** Deep transformer-based protein language models (pLMs) such as ESM-2 \cite{lin2023} capture powerful global structural, evolutionary, and biophysical distributions. However, global mean-pooled sequence embeddings can easily dilute localized, point-level mutations within catalytic triads across sequence lengths exceeding 400 to 1,000 amino acids.
3. **Annotation provenance is routinely ignored:** Annotations derived from direct low-throughput *in vitro* enzymatic assays with published crystal structures possess fundamentally different evidential weight than unvalidated electronic transfers. An anomaly score cannot be interpreted meaningfully without factoring in the epistemic strength of the underlying evidence.

### Contributions of this Work
In this study, we present the **Annotation Contradiction Atlas**, an evidence-aware relational database and triage dashboard engineered to identify, decompose, and prioritize potential annotation conflicts across the human kinome. Designed explicitly as a human-in-the-loop decision-support tool rather than an autonomous classifier, the Atlas makes four core contributions:

* **A Multi-Layered Computational Evidence Framework:** We integrate four complementary analytical modalities: global pairwise sequence alignment and $k$-mer Jaccard similarity, dense 320-dimensional ESM-2 transformer embeddings with class-conditioned centroid and $k$-NN anomaly scoring, rule-based kinase catalytic motif validation (VAIK, HRD, DFG) with structural geometry constraints, and an evidence provenance penalty model grounded in UniProt evidence ontology codes.
* **Rigorous Empirical Auditing and Evaluation:** We construct a strictly partitioned 44-case evaluation suite (Benchmark v2), consisting of 21 calibration cases, 21 independent test cases (encompassing synthetic label contradictions and catalytic knockouts), and two natural biological exceptions. We present fully audited, reproducible confusion matrices and explain why sequence similarity baselines outperformed the complex ensemble.
* **Mechanistic Diagnostic Analysis of ESM-2 Sensitivity:** We analyze the behavior of ESM-2 embeddings under systematic threshold sweeps, demonstrating why global mean pooling limits the sensitivity of small language models to localized motif knockouts and resulting in zero positive predictions at $\tau \ge 0.40$.
* **Separation of Natural Biological Exceptions from Annotation Errors:** We implement and validate a dedicated triage protocol that separates natural pseudokinases (e.g., VRK3 and ILK) from suspected annotation conflicts, preserving their diagnostic scores without misleading biocurators into flagging valid biology as corrupted database records.

## II. Background and Related Work

### A. Annotation Drift and Error Propagation in Curated Databases
The challenge of misannotation in public biological databases has been recognized for over two decades. In a landmark study, Brenner \cite{brenner1999} demonstrated that automated homology-based transfers at sequence identity thresholds below 30% yield functional error rates exceeding 30%. Devos and Valencia \cite{devos2001} subsequently demonstrated that structural similarity does not guarantee enzymatic functional conservation, as modest active-site rearrangements can radically alter enzymatic specificity. Schnoes et al. \cite{schnoes2009} conducted a comprehensive audit of functional annotations across multiple enzyme superfamilies, discovering error rates ranging from 5% to over 40% in secondary databases, with the highest error frequencies observed in computationally generated electronic annotations (evidence code `IEA`). 

Modern curation consortia have introduced standardized evidence ontologies to mitigate these issues. UniProtKB and the Gene Ontology classify all annotations using Evidence and Conclusion Ontology (ECO) codes \cite{uniprot2023, go2023}. These codes span experimental validations (e.g., `EXP`, `IDA`, `IPI`), curated phylogenetic inferences (e.g., `IBA`), author statements (`TAS`, `NAS`), and purely computational inferences (`ISS`, `IEA`). While these codes provide transparent lineage, biocurators currently lack automated tools that cross-examine the biological plausibility of an annotation against its underlying evidence code within an interactive triage workflow.

### B. Protein Kinase Structural Biology and the Pseudokinase Paradox
The eukaryotic protein kinase (ePK) catalytic domain comprises approximately 250 to 300 amino acids folded into a two-lobed tertiary structure \cite{hanks2001, manning2002}. The smaller N-terminal lobe consists predominantly of five antiparallel $\beta$-strands and an $\alpha$C-helix, while the larger C-terminal lobe is primarily $\alpha$-helical. The catalytic cleft is positioned between the two lobes and is characterized by three universally conserved sequence motifs essential for ATP binding, phosphate orientation, and phosphotransfer \cite{kornev2008, taylor2012}:

1. **Subdomain II (The VAIK Motif):** Contains an invariant lysine residue ($\text{Lys}^{72}$ in murine PKA) positioned within a $\text{V-A-I-K}$ or $\text{A-X-K}$ consensus sequence. This lysine coordinates the $\alpha$- and $\beta$-phosphates of ATP and forms a crucial salt bridge with a conserved glutamate on the $\alpha$C-helix ($\text{Glu}^{91}$ in PKA).
2. **Subdomain VIB (The HRD Motif):** Contains the catalytic loop consensus $\text{H-R-D-x-[KR]-x-x-N}$. The invariant aspartate ($\text{Asp}^{166}$ in PKA) serves as the catalytic base that accepts a proton from the substrate hydroxyl group during nucleophilic attack.
3. **Subdomain VII (The DFG Motif):** Contains the magnesium-binding consensus $\text{D-F-G}$ at the start of the activation loop. The invariant aspartate ($\text{Asp}^{184}$ in PKA) directly coordinates the primary $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ion, which chelates the $\beta$- and $\gamma$-phosphates of ATP.

Despite the stringent evolutionary conservation of these motifs across catalytically active kinases, approximately 10% of the human kinome comprises **pseudokinases** \cite{eyers2013, murphy2014, jacobsen2019}. Pseudokinases possess clear structural homology to the ePK fold but harbor natural substitutions in one or more of these invariant catalytic residues. For instance, **Vaccinia-related kinase 3 (VRK3, Q9Y616)** exhibits a degenerate HRD motif ($\text{H-G-A}$) and an altered DFG motif ($\text{S-S-G}$), rendering it catalytically inert toward conventional substrates while functioning as an essential scaffold that activates VHR phosphatase \cite{scheeff2009}. Similarly, **Integrin-linked kinase (ILK, Q13418)** contains an altered HRD motif ($\text{F-K-K}$) and DFG motif ($\text{D-F-F}$), operating primarily as a mechanical and structural hub in focal adhesions \cite{fukuda2003, wickstrom2010}. 

The existence of pseudokinases creates a profound challenge for automated curation pipelines: a motif analysis tool will correctly observe that ILK or VRK3 lacks the standard catalytic machinery, but classifying them as "erroneous database entries" is biologically incorrect. They represent natural evolutionary adaptations, not curation errors.

### C. Protein Language Models and Deep Representations
Recent breakthroughs in deep self-supervised learning have established protein language models (pLMs) as powerful engines for biological representation \cite{rives2021, elnaggar2021, lin2023}. Models such as ESM-1b and ESM-2 (Evolutionary Scale Modeling) are trained on hundreds of millions of evolutionary diverse sequences from UniRef50 using masked language modeling objectives. Through this pretraining, the transformer attention heads implicitly learn secondary structure, tertiary residue-residue contacts, evolutionary conservation, and biochemical properties directly from sequence without explicit structural inputs \cite{lin2023}.

Researchers have increasingly applied pLMs to variant effect prediction, protein design, and anomaly detection. However, standard downstream workflows frequently pool the hidden representations of all tokens across a sequence into a single mean vector. While this global embedding captures overall fold and family topology with remarkable fidelity, its mathematical formulation—an unweighted average across hundreds or thousands of residue vectors—inherently dampens the signal of single-residue or localized three-residue mutations \cite{brandes2022}. In this paper, we directly audit and quantify this architectural limitation within the context of kinase active-site verification.

### B. Relational Schema Design
The backend database is implemented in SQLite3, enforcing strict relational integrity through foreign key constraints, unique indices, and enumerations. The relational model consists of seven core tables:

1. **`proteins`**: Stores primary sequence records, including UniProt accession (primary key), entry name, gene symbol, organism (`Homo sapiens`), sequence length, raw amino acid string, and kinematic classification (e.g., Tyrosine kinase, Serine/threonine kinase, Dual-specificity).
2. **`annotations`**: Stores individual functional assertions linked to each protein via a foreign key (`protein_id`). Attributes include annotation category (`MOLECULAR_FUNCTION`, `CATALYTIC_ACTIVITY`, `SUBCELLULAR_LOCATION`), ontology term identifier (e.g., GO accession), and human-readable descriptive text.
3. **`evidence_provenance`**: Stores lineage records for each annotation. Attributes include evidence code (`EXP`, `IDA`, `IBA`, `ISS`, `IEA`, etc.), evidence category (`EXPERIMENTAL`, `PHYLOGENETIC_CURATED`, `AUTHOR_STATEMENT`, `COMPUTATIONAL`, `UNSPECIFIED`), literature reference (PubMed ID), and computed numerical reliability scores.
4. **`kinase_features`**: Stores extracted catalytic motif coordinates and status. Attributes include binary indicators and string values for VAIK, HRD, and DFG subdomains, motif start and end positions, spatial distance spans, and an overall geometric validity flag.
5. **`contradiction_scores`**: Stores multi-evidence scoring records for evaluated protein-annotation pairs. Stores individual component scores ($A_{\text{emb}}$, $A_{\text{sim}}$, $A_{\text{feat}}$, $P_{\text{prov}}$), the aggregated score $S$, and diagnostic metadata.
6. **`review_flags`**: Manages the curator triage priority queue. Attributes include review priority (`HIGH`, `MODERATE`, `LOW`), review status (`PENDING`, `IN_REVIEW`, `RESOLVED`), and explicit biological exception tagging (`STANDARD`, `PSEUDOKINASE`, `DIVERGENT_ISOFORM`).
7. **`benchmark_cases`**: Manages the Benchmark v2 evaluation partition. Attributes include case identifier, protein accession, benchmark task (`TASK1_LABEL_CONTRADICTION`, `TASK2_SEQUENCE_PERTURBATION`, `TASK3_BIOLOGICAL_EXCEPTION`), partition split (`CALIBRATION`, `INDEPENDENT_TEST`), ground-truth conflict flag, and validation notes.

## IV. Multi-Evidence Scoring Methodology and Algorithmic Formulation

The core analytical engine computes four independent discordance metrics for any protein-annotation pair, synthesizing them into a final evidence-aware review priority score $S \in [0.0, 1.0]$.

### A. Sequence Similarity and Alignment Coverage Analysis ($A_{\text{sim}}$)
Sequence homology comparison serves as the primary empirical baseline. Given a candidate query sequence $Q$ and an established reference panel $R = \{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_m\}$ sharing the candidate's annotated functional class (e.g., active Tyrosine kinases), the sequence similarity engine calculates both optimal global alignment and lexical $k$-mer overlap.

Pairwise alignment is executed using Biopython's `PairwiseAligner` implementing the Needleman-Wunsch dynamic programming algorithm \cite{needleman1970}. Sequences exceeding 1,200 amino acids are truncated to 1,200 residues to cap computational overhead:
$$\text{Alignment Parameters: } \text{Match} = +2.0, \quad \text{Mismatch} = -1.0, \quad \text{Gap Open} = -10.0, \quad \text{Gap Extend} = -0.5$$

Let $\text{score}(Q, r_j)$ denote the optimal pairwise alignment score between query $Q$ and reference $r_j$. The maximum theoretical alignment score is defined as:
$$\text{max\_score} = 2.0 \times \min(|Q|, |r_j|)$$
The alignment identity fraction is given by:
$$\text{ident}_{\text{align}} = \max\left(0.0, \frac{\text{score}(Q, r_j)}{\text{max\_score}}\right)$$

To guard against alignment artifacts in highly divergent or repetitive regions, we compute the lexical $k$-mer Jaccard similarity ($k=3$). Let $K(S)$ denote the set of all canonical 3-mer substrings extracted from sequence $S$:
$$J_{\text{kmer}}(Q, r_j) = \frac{|K(Q) \cap K(r_j)|}{|K(Q) \cup K(r_j)|}$$

The unified sequence identity metric $\text{Identity}(Q, r_j)$ is computed as a weighted combination:
$$\text{Identity}(Q, r_j) = 0.70 \cdot \text{ident}_{\text{align}} + 0.30 \cdot J_{\text{kmer}}(Q, r_j)$$

Sequence coverage is calculated based on relative sequence lengths:
$$\text{Coverage}(Q, r_j) = \frac{\min(|Q|, |r_j|)}{\max(|Q|, |r_j|)}$$
$$\text{Similarity}(Q, r_j) = \text{Identity}(Q, r_j) \times \left(0.80 + 0.20 \cdot \text{Coverage}(Q, r_j)\right)$$

The overall sequence similarity anomaly score $A_{\text{sim}} \in [0.0, 1.0]$ is derived from the maximum observed identity across the reference panel $R$:
$$\text{best\_identity} = \max_{r_j \in R} \text{Identity}(Q, r_j)$$
$$A_{\text{sim}} = 1.0 - \text{best\_identity}$$

If the query's annotated functional class directly conflicts with the verified annotations of reference sequences showing high alignment ($\text{Identity} \ge 0.40$), a label discordance flag is triggered.

### B. Dense ESM-2 Embedding Anomaly Detection ($A_{\text{emb}}$)
To capture continuous biophysical, structural, and evolutionary patterns beyond string identity, we employ the transformer-based protein language model `facebook/esm2_t6_8M_UR50D` (6 layers, 320-dimensional hidden states, 8 million parameters) \cite{lin2023}. 

#### 1) Tokenization and Sliding-Window Mean Pooling
Given a query sequence $Q$ of length $L = |Q|$, sequences with $L \le 1,022$ are tokenized with prepended `cls` and appended `eos` delimiter tokens. For sequences exceeding the maximum transformer context window ($L > 1,024$), we implement a deterministic sliding-window strategy with window size $W = 1,022$ and stride $S = 511$ (50% overlap).

Let $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{320}$ represent the final hidden-layer embedding for token $t$. Token representations corresponding to delimiter tokens are excluded. The mean-pooled sequence representation for window $w$ is:
$$\mathbf{v}_w = \frac{1}{|T_w|} \sum_{t \in T_w} h_t$$
For multi-window sequences, the sequence-level vector is obtained by averaging window vectors: $\mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{N_w} \sum_{w=1}^{N_w} \mathbf{v}_w$. The final representation $\mathbf{e}_Q$ is $L_2$-normalized:
$$\mathbf{e}_Q = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_2} \in \mathbb{R}^{320}$$

#### 2) Centroid and $k$-NN Anomaly Formulation
For a designated kinase reference class $C$, let $\mathbf{E}_C = \{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \dots, \mathbf{e}_{N_C}\}$ represent the reference embeddings. The class centroid is computed and normalized:
$$\mathbf{c}_C = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_C} \mathbf{e}_i}{\|\sum_{i=1}^{N_C} \mathbf{e}_i\|_2}$$

Given query embedding $\mathbf{e}_Q$, the cosine distance to the centroid is:
$$d_{\text{centroid}}(\mathbf{e}_Q, \mathbf{c}_C) = 1.0 - (\mathbf{e}_Q \cdot \mathbf{c}_C)$$

We also compute the local neighborhood density using $k$-nearest neighbors ($k=5$). Let $\mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{e}_Q)$ denote the $k$ nearest reference embeddings in $\mathbf{E}_C$ ranked by cosine distance:
$$d_{\text{knn}}(\mathbf{e}_Q, \mathbf{E}_C) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{\mathbf{e}_j \in \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{e}_Q)} \left(1.0 - (\mathbf{e}_Q \cdot \mathbf{e}_j)\right)$$

The combined reference distance is a weighted blend of global and local dispersion:
$$D_{\text{combined}} = 0.40 \cdot d_{\text{centroid}} + 0.60 \cdot d_{\text{knn}}$$
The base anomaly score is scaled relative to an empirical saturation threshold ($0.35$):
$$A_{\text{base}} = \min\left(1.0, \frac{D_{\text{combined}}}{0.35}\right)$$

#### 3) Class-Discordance Penalty
To identify label misassignments (e.g., a Serine/Threonine kinase mislabeled as a Tyrosine kinase), we compute the minimum distance from $\mathbf{e}_Q$ to any reference embedding within its annotated class ($\min_{\mathbf{e} \in \mathbf{E}_{\text{own}}} d_{\text{cos}}$) versus an alternate kinase class ($\min_{\mathbf{e} \in \mathbf{E}_{\text{other}}} d_{\text{cos}}$). If the query embedding is substantially closer to an opposing class:
$$\Delta_{\text{class}} = \min_{\mathbf{e} \in \mathbf{E}_{\text{own}}} d_{\text{cos}}(\mathbf{e}_Q, \mathbf{e}) - \min_{\mathbf{e} \in \mathbf{E}_{\text{other}}} d_{\text{cos}}(\mathbf{e}_Q, \mathbf{e})$$
$$\text{Penalty}_{\text{class}} = \begin{cases} 
\min\left(0.30, \Delta_{\text{class}} \times 3.0\right) & \text{if } \Delta_{\text{class}} > 0.02 \\ 
0.0 & \text{otherwise} 
\end{cases}$$

The final ESM-2 embedding anomaly score is:
$$A_{\text{emb}} = \min\left(1.0, A_{\text{base}} + \text{Penalty}_{\text{class}}\right)$$

### C. Kinase Catalytic Motif and Spatial Ordering Verification ($A_{\text{feat}}$)
While language models evaluate continuous sequence space, enzymatic competence requires strict, discrete spatial arrangements of chemical functional groups. The feature extraction engine scans sequence $Q$ for the three canonical kinase subdomains using regular expressions:

* **Subdomain II (VAIK Motif):** Regular expression `[LIVAM]A[IV]K`. Variants matching `[LIVAM].[IV]K` or `.[IV]K` are classified as non-canonical variants.
* **Subdomain VIB (HRD Motif):** Regular expression `[HY]RD[LIVMFY]K..N` or `HRD[LIVMFY]`. Variants matching `[HY]RD` or `.RD` are classified as non-canonical variants.
* **Subdomain VII (DFG Motif):** Regular expression `DFG` or `[DNS]FG`. Variants matching `D[FY]G` or `.FG` are classified as non-canonical variants.

Let $N_{\text{canon}} \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ denote the number of strictly canonical motifs identified, and $N_{\text{variant}} \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ denote the number of non-canonical variants. The base motif completeness score is:
$$\text{Score}_{\text{base}} = \frac{1.0 \cdot N_{\text{canon}} + 0.45 \cdot N_{\text{variant}}}{3.0}$$

Furthermore, we enforce strict **spatial geometric ordering**. In an intact eukaryotic kinase domain, these subdomains must occur sequentially along the primary sequence and satisfy physical distance constraints:
$$\text{Condition: } \text{pos}(\text{VAIK}) < \text{pos}(\text{HRD}) < \text{pos}(\text{DFG})$$
$$\text{Span Constraints: } 40 \le (\text{pos}(\text{HRD}) - \text{pos}(\text{VAIK})) \le 300 \quad \text{and} \quad 10 \le (\text{pos}(\text{DFG}) - \text{pos}(\text{HRD})) \le 80$$

If any identified motifs violate sequential ordering or span constraints, a spatial penalty multiplier of $0.85$ is applied:
$$\text{Score}_{\text{feat}} = \begin{cases} 
\text{Score}_{\text{base}} & \text{if spatial ordering and span constraints are satisfied} \\ 
0.85 \cdot \text{Score}_{\text{base}} & \text{if spatial ordering or span constraints are violated} 
\end{cases}$$
The kinase feature anomaly score is defined as:
$$A_{\text{feat}} = 1.0 - \text{Score}_{\text{feat}}$$

### D. Annotation Evidence Provenance Penalty ($P_{\text{prov}}$)
The evidential strength of an annotation modulates the urgency of any computational contradiction. An atypical sequence feature in an entry supported by multiple peer-reviewed crystal structures suggests specialized biology, whereas the same feature in an entry inferred via automated computational transfer (`IEA`) suggests a probable database error.

The provenance engine maps UniProt ECO evidence codes to categorical reliability weights $W_{\text{cat}}$:
* **`EXPERIMENTAL` (`EXP`, `IDA`, `IPI`, `IMP`, `IGI`, `IEP`):** Weight $= 0.95$ (minimum baseline $0.80$)
* **`PHYLOGENETIC_CURATED` (`IBA`, `IBD`, `IKR`, `IRD`):** Weight $= 0.70$ (minimum baseline $0.55$)
* **`AUTHOR_STATEMENT` (`TAS`, `NAS`):** Weight $= 0.50$ (minimum baseline $0.40$)
* **`COMPUTATIONAL` (`ISS`, `ISO`, `ISA`, `ISM`, `IEA`):** Weight $= 0.35$ (minimum baseline $0.20$)
* **`UNSPECIFIED` (Missing or legacy record):** Weight $= 0.25$ (minimum baseline $0.10$)

A verification bonus ($+0.05$) is credited if the annotation links directly to an indexed PubMed bibliographic record. The composite provenance reliability $R_{\text{prov}} \in [0.10, 1.00]$ is computed across all supporting evidence records, and the provenance penalty is formulated as:
$$P_{\text{prov}} = 1.0 - R_{\text{prov}}$$

### E. Evidence-Aware Ensemble Aggregation and Safeguards
The unified contradiction score $S \in [0.0, 1.0]$ aggregates all four evidence streams via linear combination:
$$S = w_{\text{emb}} \cdot A_{\text{emb}} + w_{\text{sim}} \cdot A_{\text{sim}} + w_{\text{feat}} \cdot A_{\text{feat}} + w_{\text{prov}} \cdot P_{\text{prov}}$$
where the calibrated component weights are:
$$w_{\text{emb}} = 0.35, \quad w_{\text{sim}} = 0.25, \quad w_{\text{feat}} = 0.25, \quad w_{\text{prov}} = 0.15 \quad \left(\sum w = 1.00\right)$$

#### Heuristic Safeguards
To prevent common false-positive failure modes, the scoring engine enforces three deterministic safeguards:
1. **Safeguard 1 (Canonical Structure Damping):** If a protein sequence exhibits intact canonical catalytic motifs ($A_{\text{feat}} \le 0.15$) and low language-model anomaly ($A_{\text{emb}} \le 0.40$), an elevated score driven solely by weak provenance is capped at $S \le 0.55$, preventing false `HIGH` priority escalation.
2. **Safeguard 2 (Non-Kinase / Extreme Outlier Escalation):** If a sequence lacks all kinase motifs ($A_{\text{feat}} \ge 0.85$) and exhibits severe sequence divergence ($A_{\text{sim}} \ge 0.70$), priority is forced to `HIGH` regardless of provenance.
3. **Safeguard 3 (Biological Exception Decoupling):** If a sequence is identified as an established natural pseudokinase (e.g., VRK3, ILK), its score is labeled as an *evolutionary diagnostic divergence score*, and the entry is diverted from the standard suspected-conflict queue into a dedicated exception queue.

Review priority thresholds are calibrated as:
$$\text{Priority} = \begin{cases} 
\text{HIGH} & \text{if } S \ge 0.65 \\ 
\text{MODERATE} & \text{if } 0.40 \le S < 0.65 \\ 
\text{LOW} & \text{if } S < 0.40 
\end{cases}$$

Evaluating annotation contradiction detection is notoriously challenging because public biological databases lack standardized ground-truth labels for erroneous entries. Most historical database errors were silently overwritten during periodic re-annotations without formal deprecation logs. We introduce targeted *in silico* alanine substitutions into invariant catalytic residues (e.g., mutating the catalytic aspartate in the HRD motif to alanine, or mutating the DFG triad to AAA) across 8 distinct kinase sequences, evaluated alongside the 9 negative controls.

### D. Evaluation Metrics and Mathematical Conventions
System predictions are evaluated against ground truth using standard binary classification metrics: True Positives ($TP$), False Positives ($FP$), True Negatives ($TN$), and False Negatives ($FN$). The mathematical formulations are defined as follows:

$$\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad \text{Recall (Sensitivity)} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}$$
$$F_1\text{-score} = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} = \frac{2 \cdot TP}{2 \cdot TP + FP + FN}$$
$$\text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}, \qquad \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + TN + FN}$$

#### Zero-Division and Undefined Handling Conventions
In bioinformatics benchmark evaluations where models exhibit extreme conservative behavior, situations arise where $TP + FP = 0$ (no positive predictions). In such instances, precision is mathematically indeterminate ($0/0$). Across our evaluation scripts and reporting tables:
* If $TP = 0$ and $FP = 0$, precision is reported as **Undefined** (or null in JSON artifacts), and $F_1$-score is mathematically **Undefined**.
* If $FP > 0$ but $TP = 0$ (as in Task 2 for ESM-2), Precision is strictly $0.0000$, Recall is $0.0000$, and $F_1$-score is strictly $0.0000$.

## VII. In-Depth Discussion and Mechanistic Analysis

### A. Why Sequence Similarity Outperformed Deep Language Representations
A central, unexpected outcome of this study is that standard pairwise sequence alignment and $k$-mer overlap ($F_1 = 0.6000$) substantially outperformed both dense ESM-2 embeddings ($F_1 = 0.2667$) and the multi-evidence ensemble ($F_1 = 0.2667$). Understanding the mechanistic basis of this performance discrepancy provides valuable insights for biological machine learning:

1. **The Dilution Problem of Global Mean Pooling:** The transformer architecture computes contextual representations for individual residues, but downstream anomaly scoring averages these vectors across all sequence positions ($\mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{|T|} \sum h_t$). In a human kinase of 450 to 900 amino acids, an *in silico* catalytic knockout (e.g., mutating $\text{Asp}^{166} \to \text{Ala}$) alters 1 to 3 residues—less than $0.5\%$ of the total sequence length. Because the remaining 99.5% of the sequence preserves the overall kinase fold, the global cosine distance between wild-type and mutant embeddings shifts by only $0.02$ to $0.05$. At a calibrated threshold of $0.1814$, this shift is insufficient to trigger an anomaly flag, explaining why ESM-2 achieved $0.0\%$ recall in Task 2.
2. **Direct Lexical Sensitivity of Alignment Scoring:** In contrast to global vector averaging, Needleman-Wunsch alignment and 3-mer Jaccard scoring directly penalize mismatched substrings. When a key functional region is corrupted, the local alignment score drops immediately, and unshared 3-mers directly reduce the Jaccard coefficient $J_{\text{kmer}}$.
3. **Reference Panel Sparsity:** Our reference panels comprised 20 to 40 curated kinase exemplars. While sufficient for global homology matching, estimating high-dimensional (320-D) Gaussian or $k$-NN reference manifolds from small sample sizes leads to substantial variance at distribution boundaries.

### B. Pathology of Naive Ensemble Weighting
The multi-evidence ensemble failed to improve upon the sequence similarity baseline because fixed linear combination behaves as an unweighted compromise. When a candidate exhibits a subtle contradiction:
* In Task 1 (Label Swaps), the sequence similarity module flagged high discordance ($A_{\text{sim}} \approx 0.60$), but the kinase feature module registered $A_{\text{feat}} \approx 0.00$ (since catalytic motifs were unaltered) and provenance penalty was minimal ($P_{\text{prov}} \approx 0.05$). The resulting weighted sum remained below the $0.40$ threshold, suppressing the alarm.
* In Task 2 (Sequence Perturbations), kinase features registered high discordance, but ESM-2 registered low discordance, once again pulling the composite score below the threshold.

This demonstrates that linear ensemble models are fundamentally ill-suited for multi-modal contradiction triage; rather, triage systems require **asymmetric logical disjunctions** (e.g., flagging an entry if *either* motif integrity *or* sequence classification fails decisively, conditioned on provenance).

### C. The Biocuration Triage Paradigm
Our results reinforce the conceptual boundary between *computational anomaly detection* and *biocuration triage*. In traditional machine learning, false positives are treated as statistical errors to be eliminated. In biocuration, a high anomaly score is simply a flag indicating that an entry deviates from established reference norms. As demonstrated by pseudokinases VRK3 and ILK, high discordance can signify either an error *or* an extraordinary biological adaptation. By decoupling these cases into dedicated interface views, the Annotation Contradiction Atlas preserves the diagnostic value of computational models without corrupting database integrity.

## VIII. Limitations and Threats to Validity

To ensure complete transparency and scientific rigor, we explicitly outline five principal limitations of this study:

1. **Restricted Kinome Subsetting:** The current database is populated with 500 reviewed human protein kinases from Swiss-Prot. It does not yet encompass the non-human kinomes (e.g., mouse, zebrafish, yeast) or unreviewed TrEMBL entries where annotation contradictions are hypothesized to occur at substantially higher frequencies.
2. **Small Reference Panel Dimensions:** Reference panels utilized for $k$-NN distance calculations contained between 20 and 40 sequences. In outlier kinases such as ANKK1 (Q8NFD2), the nearest available reference match in the panel exhibited low baseline sequence identity ($3.4\%$). Future iterations must scale reference panels to encompass all known subfamilies within the human kinome.
3. **Synthetic Nature of Evaluation Benchmarks:** Due to the historical scarcity of systematically documented curation errors, Benchmark v2 relies primarily on *in silico* label swaps and catalytic motif knockouts. While biologically grounded, synthetic perturbations may not fully capture the nuanced errors introduced by automated text-mining or cross-species phylogenetic transfers.
4. **Heuristic Linear Ensemble Weighting:** The weights in the ensemble equation ($0.35, 0.25, 0.25, 0.15$) were calibrated empirically on a 21-case training set. As established in Section VII-B, linear combinations cannot capture conditional dependencies between sequence integrity and functional classification.
5. **Model Parameter Scale:** We evaluated the lightweight `esm2_t6_8M_UR50D` model (8 million parameters) to enable interactive CPU-based inference. Larger foundation models (e.g., ESM-2 650M or 3B parameters) may exhibit enhanced zero-shot sensitivity to localized active-site mutations, though at significantly higher computational cost.

## X. Conclusion and Future Directions

The Annotation Contradiction Atlas establishes an evidence-aware, transparent framework for prioritizing potentially inconsistent protein kinase annotations in biological databases. Through rigorous empirical evaluation on Benchmark v2, we demonstrated that standard sequence similarity baselines ($F_1 = 0.6000$) significantly outperformed both dense ESM-2 transformer embeddings ($F_1 = 0.2667$) and multi-evidence linear ensembles ($F_1 = 0.2667$). Diagnostic sweeps revealed that global mean-pooled language model representations dilute localized catalytic active-site corruptions, resulting in zero positive predictions at anomaly thresholds $\tau \ge 0.40$. Furthermore, we demonstrated that natural pseudokinases such as VRK3 and ILK must be systematically segregated from suspected annotation conflicts to prevent legitimate evolutionary adaptations from polluting curator triage queues.

Future research will advance in three directions:
1. **Per-Residue Attention Pooling:** Replacing global sequence mean-pooling with localized attention-weighted pooling focused specifically on catalytic triads (subdomains II, VIB, and VII), restoring language model sensitivity to single-residue mutations.
2. **Non-Linear Decision Logic:** Transitioning from linear ensemble weighting to non-linear, decision-tree or Bayesian belief networks that model asymmetric dependencies between motif presence and functional evidence.
3. **Active Learning in Live Biocuration:** Deploying the Atlas within active biocuration environments, allowing curator feedback to refine anomaly thresholds dynamically across diverse eukaryotic enzyme superfamilies.

## Appendix A: Formal Metric Formulations

To ensure mathematical precision, all evaluation formulas used throughout the Atlas are formally summarized:

1. **Jaccard $k$-mer Similarity:**
   $$J_k(S_1, S_2) = \frac{|\{s \in S_1 : |s|=k\} \cap \{s \in S_2 : |s|=k\}|}{|\{s \in S_1 : |s|=k\} \cup \{s \in S_2 : |s|=k\}|}$$
2. **Cosine Distance:**
   $$d_{\text{cos}}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = 1.0 - \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2}$$
3. **Class Centroid:**
   $$\mathbf{c} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{e}_i}{\|\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{e}_i\|_2}, \quad \text{where } \mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{320}$$
4. **Binary Classification Metrics:**
   $$\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \quad \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}$$
   $$F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot TP}{2 \cdot TP + FP + FN}$$
   $$\text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}, \quad \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + TN + FN}$$
